This person was a Jewish prisoner in a Nazi forced-labor camp during the Holocaust.

From the testimony, he describes being subjected to extreme abuse: prisoners were forced to stand naked in winter, crawl in snow, work 10–12 hour factory shifts, live without proper washing facilities or underwear, eat very little, and sleep on crude wooden bunks. People regularly died during the night, and some prisoners killed themselves by touching the camp’s electrified fence.

He also describes systematic humiliation outside the camp. Local people insulted the prisoners, and even children were allowed to kick them and throw stones at them. His main point is that the Nazis were not only killing people physically, but deliberately stripping them of their sense of being human.

The passage does not tell us what ultimately happened to him after this period, but because he is giving this testimony afterward, he clearly survived the camp and the war.

The most important moments are the ones that show both the physical brutality of the camp and the deeper process of dehumanization.

The guard took pleasure in killing prisoners. KM says the man especially targeted “the big and the tall ones,” treating murder almost as a personal challenge. This shows that violence was not only disciplinary but could become sadistic entertainment.

Prisoners were deliberately tortured after exhausting labor. After working 10–12 hours in a factory, they could be forced into nighttime “gymnastics,” including standing naked in winter and crawling through snow.

Living conditions were extremely degrading. About 2,000 prisoners lived in barracks with almost no opportunity to wash, no underwear, poor food, and primitive sleeping arrangements on three-tier wooden shelves.

Death became part of ordinary daily life. KM describes waking up and finding that someone nearby had died during the night. The striking detail is that prisoners sometimes wondered whether the dead were “better off” because they no longer had to face another day.

Suicide was common and frighteningly simple. Some prisoners deliberately touched the camp’s high-voltage fence. Every morning, a prisoner unit collected the bodies with a cart. This is one of the strongest images in the testimony because it shows how hopeless conditions had become.

Escape felt almost impossible. Even if someone escaped physically, KM asks where they could go: they were emaciated, wearing striped prison uniforms, unable to speak the local language, and surrounded by a society in which many people were hostile or unwilling to help.

Civilians also participated in humiliation. During marches to the factory, people shouted abuse, while children kicked prisoners and threw stones at them. This mattered deeply to KM because it showed that the prisoners were being treated as something less than human.

The most important idea comes at the end. KM concludes that killing may not be the worst crime; degrading and dehumanizing a person can be even more profound. That final reflection gives meaning to everything before it. The testimony is not only about suffering and death, but about how a system tried to destroy prisoners’ sense of human dignity.

He was imprisoned in a German labor camp during the war and subjected to a system designed not only to exploit his labor but to strip away ordinary human life.

He describes being forced to work 10–12 hours in a factory, then being made to perform “gymnastics,” crawl naked in snow, stand for long periods in the Appellplatz, live without proper washing facilities or underwear, eat inadequate food, and sleep on rough wooden shelves. Death was so common that people would wake to find prisoners beside them had died during the night. Suicide was also common, with prisoners killing themselves on the electrified camp fence.

He also witnessed deliberate cruelty from a camp authority figure who, according to KM, enjoyed killing people and treated selecting “the big and the tall ones” as a kind of challenge. Outside the camp, civilians and even children insulted, kicked, and threw stones at the prisoners as they marched to work.

But the testimony gradually shifts from describing what was physically done to him to something more difficult to measure: what happened to his sense of being human. A particularly important movement is from “you wondered, maybe they're better off than you are” to “you weren't human anymore” and finally to his conclusion that killing may not be the greatest crime, because there is another violence in degrading and dehumanizing a person before death.

So what happened to him was not simply imprisonment, forced labor, hunger, cold, and exposure to death. The testimony suggests that he experienced an environment in which the boundaries between living and dying, human and animal, hope and resignation were deliberately eroded.

One detail worth noticing when returning to the passage is that he repeatedly says “we had to”: “we had to get out,” “we had to stand,” “we had to crawl.” The grammar itself accumulates coercion. By the time he reaches the final statement, “that’s what we were,” his account has moved from a sequence of forced actions to a description of what those actions were trying to make the prisoners become.

what are the most important moments or details in this testimony
AI
Several moments carry unusual weight because they do more than report suffering. They reveal how the camp reorganized the prisoner’s relation to body, death, escape, and personhood.

• The perpetrator’s pleasure in killing. KM does not describe violence as merely bureaucratic or impersonal. He remembers a man who “got pleasure out of killing people” and who treated choosing “the big and the tall ones” as a “challenge.”

• The forced “gymnastics” after 10–12 hours of labor. The word “gymnastics” is important because it grotesquely renames punishment as exercise. After exhausting factory work, prisoners were forced to stand naked in winter and crawl in the snow.

• Waking beside the dead. One of the most powerful moments is when KM says that in the morning there was “always one or so that didn’t make the night,” followed by the thought that perhaps they were “better off.” Death is no longer only feared. It begins to appear as possible relief.

• The electrified fence and the morning collection of bodies. Suicide becomes part of the camp’s daily landscape. The detail of a special prisoner commando pushing a cart to collect the bodies turns individual despair into routine.

• The impossibility of escape even beyond the fence. KM’s question, “where would you go to?”, widens the camp beyond its physical boundaries.

• Children kicking prisoners and throwing stones. This is perhaps one of the most unsettling details because the violence is not limited to guards. Children participate, apparently with adult permission.

The testimony culminates in KM’s own interpretation: “killing a human being really isn’t the biggest crime. It’s to degrade him, to-- to dehumanize him.”

One detail I would especially return to is the shift in his pronouns. He moves between “we,” “you,” and “I.” At times he tells his own experience as though it could only be spoken through an impersonal “you”: “you woke up,” “you wondered,” “you felt like.” That may be more than conversational habit. It gives the testimony a strange double quality: intensely personal, yet describing a condition that had become collective.

So if I were annotating the source, I would not highlight only the dramatic words like “killing,” “suicide,” or “high-voltage wire.” I would also underline the quieter phrases: “what he called,” “better off,” “all you had to do,” “where would you go,” “allowed,” “had to.” Those phrases reveal how the camp altered the meanings of ordinary actions and ordinary language.

If a reader remembers only one thing, it should be this: for KM, the deepest violence of the camp was not only that people were killed, but that they were systematically made to feel less than human before death.

That idea gathers almost every detail in the testimony: forced nakedness, crawling in snow, exhaustion after factory labor, inadequate food, sleeping beside the dead, suicide becoming routine, children being permitted to throw stones, and the sense that even escape would lead nowhere.

The sentence to carry away is: “It’s to degrade him, to-- to dehumanize him.”

What matters is that dehumanization here is not an abstract concept added afterward. The testimony shows it happening through many small and repeated acts, until the prisoner’s relation to survival itself changes. That is what makes the passage especially difficult: the violence reaches beyond the body and begins to alter what being alive means.

Could technologies that close explanations too quickly also make us close our own knowledge and experience too early?

AI can give us information quickly and clearly. But if we depend on this way of knowing too much, it may also change how we understand, experience, and explore the world.
